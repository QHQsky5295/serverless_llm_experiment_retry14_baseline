\begin{abstract}
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in multi-tenant and domain-specific applications with bursty and skewed demand, making serverless platforms attractive for elastic, pay-as-you-go inference. LoRA allows many task- or tenant-specific adapters to share a common backbone model, but serverless multi-LoRA inference introduces a service-readiness gap: scale-out may add compute replicas before the selected replicas can execute the adapters required by incoming requests. We call this condition \emph{adapter--replica mismatch}. It exposes adapter preparation to the TTFT-critical path, weakens the latency and throughput gains of scale-out, and increases contention with KV-cache usage and batched inference. To address this problem, we present PrimeLoRA, a scaling-aware serverless system for multi-LoRA LLM inference. PrimeLoRA integrates LoRA Hit-Aware Request Placement and Scaling, Hierarchical LoRA Adapter Residency and Dynamic Migration, and Coordinated Resource Control for LoRA Loading and Inference to align adapter readiness with routing, scale-out, residency, and online inference. We implement PrimeLoRA on a multi-GPU prototype and evaluate it using Azure LLM inference traces and ShareGPT prompts. The results show that PrimeLoRA improves lifecycle cost efficiency and reduces readiness-related delay by aligning elastic capacity with executable adapter state, while exposing the latency tradeoff against always-on serverful runtimes.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in customer service, financial analysis, and domain-specific assistants, where inference demand is often bursty, concurrent, and multi-tenant. Serverless platforms are therefore attractive for elastic, pay-as-you-go inference because resources can be provisioned and reclaimed according to workload intensity~\cite{ServerlessLLM}. Serving diverse tasks with separate monolithic deployments, however, is inefficient. LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) injects low-rank updates into a frozen backbone LLM to derive task-specific adapters at modest overhead~\cite{LoRA}, enabling many tasks or tenants to share the same backbone.

In multi-LoRA inference, reducing startup overhead often means keeping the backbone model resident in ready GPU replicas or pre-staging it in nearby storage, whereas LoRA adapters are commonly loaded on demand~\cite{SLoRA}. Consequently, scale-out does not by itself imply service-ready capacity. After additional execution capacity has been provisioned, a request can begin generation on a selected replica only after its target adapter has been fetched, placed into an executable memory tier, and made GPU-ready on that replica. When adapter readiness is not prepared in advance, cross-tier transfer and on-replica adapter loading can enter the TTFT-critical path.

Figure~\ref{fig:intro_teaser} illustrates the serverless-style cost-efficiency opportunity under the representative Llama-2 7B main workload and the same GPU budget. PrimeLoRA occupies the low-cost/high-CE region, while strong always-on runtimes provide competitive latency at higher lifecycle cost. This opportunity motivates serverless multi-LoRA serving; the following paragraphs and Motivation section then isolate the adapter-readiness mechanism that makes elastic multi-LoRA serving nontrivial.

This issue becomes more pronounced when cross-replica routing and elastic scaling interact with time-varying adapter popularity. Under bursty arrivals and hotspot migration, a request may be routed to an active replica that lacks the required adapter, while a newly activated replica may still need to prepare likely-needed adapters before it can absorb demand. We refer to this recurrent condition as \emph{adapter--replica mismatch}. Although LoRA adapters are smaller than the backbone model, repeated adapter loading can still delay service readiness, increase TTFT and tail latency, and contend with KV-cache usage and batched inference.

Existing mechanisms improve important parts of this serving path, but they do not by themselves close the service-readiness gap between replica activation and adapter execution. General-purpose autoscaling policies mainly react to coarse-grained load signals such as resource utilization or request concurrency and are therefore insensitive to adapter readiness during routing and scale-out~\cite{GameTheoryScheduling}. Adapter-centric serving systems improve reuse through caching, paging, or adapter-aware scheduling, yet under elastic scale-out, local adapter reuse may not occur on the replicas that receive bursty requests for shifting hotspots. As a result, adapter locality may fail to translate into service-ready capacity, leaving routed requests exposed to additional TTFT delay~\cite{SLoRA,Chameleon}. Moreover, adapter management is tightly coupled with online inference because adapters, the KV cache, and batched execution contend for the same GPU memory and bandwidth~\cite{FASTLIBRA}. Without readiness-aware admission, promoting adapters can disturb the active working set and degrade TTFT as well as tail latency.

Our goal is to improve service readiness in dynamic serverless multi-LoRA inference, reducing TTFT and tail latency without sacrificing resource efficiency. This requires solving three coupled problems: routing requests to replicas with timely adapter executability, maintaining hierarchical adapter residency under limited GPU memory, and coordinating adapter movement with online inference when scale-out and steady-state serving share GPU memory and bandwidth.

To address these challenges, we propose PrimeLoRA, a scaling-aware serverless system for multi-LoRA LLM inference. PrimeLoRA treats adapter readiness as a service-path property and aligns request placement, scale-out preparation, hierarchical residency, and GPU-admission control around timely adapter execution. LoRA Hit-Aware Request Placement and Scaling steers requests toward more adapter-ready replicas and prepares newly activated replicas for likely-needed adapters. Hierarchical LoRA Adapter Residency and Dynamic Migration maintains adapter placement across GPU memory, host memory, and local NVMe storage according to memory budget, access popularity, and expected hit benefit. Coordinated Resource Control for LoRA Loading and Inference admits adapter movement only when it is compatible with KV-cache usage and batched inference.

Our main contributions are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
\item We identify \emph{adapter--replica mismatch} as a recurring source of latency inflation in serverless multi-LoRA inference: requests may reach active or newly activated replicas before the required adapters become executable, causing repeated adapter preparation to amplify TTFT-critical delay and degrade tail latency. Through empirical analysis on realistic workloads, we show that this effect becomes particularly pronounced under dynamic scaling.
\item We propose PrimeLoRA, a scaling-aware serverless system for multi-LoRA LLM inference that treats adapter readiness as a service-path property across routing, scale-out, residency, and online inference. PrimeLoRA couples LoRA Hit-Aware Request Placement and Scaling, Hierarchical LoRA Adapter Residency and Dynamic Migration, and Coordinated Resource Control for LoRA Loading and Inference, allowing ready-replica capacity and adapter locality to reduce readiness delay without shifting the bottleneck to online inference.
\item We implement PrimeLoRA on a multi-GPU prototype and evaluate it using the Azure LLM Inference Trace and ShareGPT prompts. Beyond aggregate latency, throughput, and cost efficiency, we decompose TTFT into scale-out-affected and GPU-ready requests, enabling a more faithful characterization of startup overhead, adapter readiness, and scaling behavior in dynamic multi-LoRA inference.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and Motivation}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Serverless Multi-LoRA Inference}

Serverless multi-LoRA inference combines elastic replica management with adapter-based model specialization. A shared backbone model is reused across many task- or tenant-specific LoRA adapters, while serving capacity is activated and reclaimed according to workload pressure. This setting differs from conventional online LLM serving because service readiness depends not only on whether a runtime replica is available, but also on whether the requested adapter is prepared on or near the selected replica. A replica may therefore be compute-ready but adapter-cold, and an adapter may be logically available but still far from GPU execution. As request arrivals and adapter popularity shift over time, the system must coordinate elastic scaling with adapter placement and residency, making adapter-aware readiness control a first-class mechanism in serverless multi-LoRA serving.

\subsection{Serverless Inference Workflow for Multi-LoRA LLMs}

A serverless multi-LoRA request follows four stages. First, the request arrives with a prompt and a target adapter identity, and the control plane decides whether existing replicas suffice or scale-out is needed. Second, the scheduler selects a replica according to serving state, resource usage, and availability~\cite{AdaptiveTransmissionScheduling}. Third, the target adapter is made executable on the selected replica, possibly through REMOTE-to-NVMe fetch, HOST promotion, or GPU loading. Fourth, generation runs with the backbone model, KV cache, and batching state; after completion, the adapter may be retained, demoted, or evicted according to later demand and resource pressure.

This workflow shows that adapter state is part of the serving path rather than a passive storage attribute. The selected replica, the adapter residency tier, and the available GPU memory jointly determine whether a request can quickly enter generation. The following subsections analyze three consequences of this workflow: mismatch between replicas and adapters during scaling, latency amplification caused by cross-tier adapter fetching, and resource contention between adapter loading and online inference.

\subsection{Adapter--Replica Mismatch under Scaling}

Serverless inference relies on elastic scaling to absorb bursty demand. In conventional serving, a newly activated replica is often treated as additional execution capacity once its runtime becomes available. This assumption is incomplete in serverless multi-LoRA inference. A replica can be compute-ready while still lacking the adapters required by the requests routed to it. Therefore, scale-out does not necessarily translate into service-ready capacity.

This gap leads to \emph{adapter--replica mismatch}. During bursts, the scheduler may route requests to active replicas that do not contain the target adapters, or hand off requests to newly activated replicas before their likely-needed adapters become executable. These requests must wait for adapter preparation before generation starts. As a result, the latency benefit of scale-out is delayed, and the requests affected by scaling can experience higher TTFT than requests whose adapters are already GPU-ready.

This mismatch makes adapter readiness a first-order scheduling concern. A policy that considers only runtime availability or queue length may increase the number of replicas without immediately improving effective serving capacity. In serverless multi-LoRA inference, scaling decisions therefore need to be evaluated by whether the added replicas can serve the adapters requested by the incoming workload, not only by whether the runtimes have been activated.

\subsection{Latency Amplification from Cross-Tier Adapter Fetching}

LoRA adapters are much smaller than backbone checkpoints, but their residency tier still has a direct effect on first-token latency. When the target adapter is already resident in GPU memory, the request can enter generation without additional adapter I/O. When the adapter resides in the HOST tier, local NVMe storage, or the REMOTE tier, the runtime must move it through one or more tiers before it becomes executable. Thus, adapter readiness is not a binary hit-or-miss property; it depends on the distance between the adapter's current tier and the GPU execution tier.

This tier distance becomes important under dynamic multi-LoRA workloads. Adapter popularity can shift over time, so adapters that were previously cold may become hot, while stale GPU-resident adapters continue to occupy upper-tier memory. If hot adapters remain in lower tiers, successive requests continue to pay cross-tier loading cost. This increases TTFT even when the backbone runtime is warm and the replica has already been selected.

Adapter placement should therefore be treated as a latency-control problem under constrained memory. A practical system must distinguish frequently reused adapters from first-touch and long-gap adapters as popularity shifts over time. Existing shared-backbone runtimes already expose this pressure under the same replay: adapter identity and reuse distance are workload facts, even when the runtime does not export a detailed cache-tier label.

\subsection{LoRA Adapter Loading--Inference Contention}

Adapter loading is not isolated from online inference. During serving, a runtime must accommodate the backbone model, active LoRA adapters, KV cache, and batched execution state within the same GPU memory and bandwidth budget. Promoting an adapter to GPU memory may reduce future misses, but the promotion itself can consume memory headroom and introduce transient I/O pressure. If this process is not coordinated with in-flight inference, improving adapter locality may still increase latency for current requests.

This contention is particularly pronounced when elastic scale-out and steady-state serving coexist. Newly activated replicas may require adapter preparation, while existing replicas continue to serve requests and maintain active KV-cache state. Under bursty arrivals, adapter loading, prefill, decode, and batching can overlap in time. A policy that only maximizes adapter residency may disturb the working set and degrade TTFT, TPOT, or end-to-end latency. Conversely, an overly conservative policy may leave frequently requested adapters in slower tiers, causing repeated loading delay.

This tradeoff shows that adapter management must be coordinated with online inference rather than optimized independently. The system must decide when adapter movement is beneficial, when it should be deferred, and how much upper-tier capacity should be preserved for the KV cache and batched execution. Such coordination is necessary to reduce both adapter-miss latency and resource interference under dynamic serverless workloads. We evaluate this coordination effect through controlled PrimeLoRA ablations in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}, rather than relying on loading-pressure metrics that are not uniformly observable across all baselines.

\section{System Overview}

\subsection{System Goal and Online Decisions}

At time $t$, let $R_t$ denote the active request set, $I_t$ the set of serving replicas, and $A$ the set of LoRA adapters. Each request $r \in R_t$ is associated with a target adapter $a(r) \in A$, an input length $x_r$, and an expected output length $y_r$. PrimeLoRA makes three classes of online decisions: request placement $z_t$, replica scaling $s_t$, and adapter residency $\eta_t$ across GPU memory, the HOST tier, and local NVMe storage.

The design objective is captured by an online serving cost that combines first-token latency, end-to-end latency, and serving cost:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
J_t(z_t,s_t,\eta_t)
=& \sum_{r \in R_t}
\left(\operatorname{TTFT}_r
+ \alpha \operatorname{E2E}_r\right) + \lambda C_t 
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:system_goal}
\end{equation}

Here, $\operatorname{TTFT}_r$ is the time from request arrival to the first generated token, $\operatorname{E2E}_r$ is end-to-end request latency, and $C_t$ is the serving cost induced by replica activation, adapter movement, and online execution. The weights $\alpha$ and $\lambda$ balance end-to-end latency and serving cost against first-token responsiveness. PrimeLoRA does not solve Eq.~\ref{eq:system_goal} offline; instead, it uses online control to align request placement, scale-out preparation, adapter residency, and GPU admission around adapter readiness under the shared GPU budget.

\subsection{System Architecture and Workflow of PrimeLoRA}

PrimeLoRA consists of a workload front end, an autoscaling controller, a request router, a hierarchical adapter residency manager, and a resource coordinator. The front end receives prompts, token-length metadata, and target adapters. The autoscaling controller manages replica activation, the router selects replicas using adapter readiness and runtime state, the residency manager tracks adapters across GPU, HOST, NVMe, and REMOTE tiers, and the resource coordinator regulates adapter loading with KV-cache usage and batched inference.

The serving workflow proceeds as follows. When a request arrives, PrimeLoRA updates workload statistics and adapter-access information. If current capacity is insufficient, the autoscaling controller may activate additional replicas through scale-out. The router evaluates candidate replicas using adapter state and runtime load, and assigns the request to a replica with lower expected service delay. If the target adapter is not GPU-ready on the selected replica, the residency manager locates the nearest available tier and prepares a backend-addressable local path. The resource coordinator then decides whether proactive GPU admission is safe under the memory budget shared by LoRA adapters, the KV cache, and batched execution. If not, PrimeLoRA defers GPU residency while still allowing the backend to resolve the adapter from a lower local tier.

Placement and scaling align requests with replicas likely to become adapter-ready, residency control shortens the path from adapter storage to execution, and resource coordination prevents locality gains from being offset by inference interference. Together, these mechanisms reduce the service-readiness gap between elastic compute capacity and executable adapter state.

\section{System Design of PrimeLoRA}

\subsection{LoRA Hit-Aware Request Placement and Scaling}

PrimeLoRA improves adapter readiness at request placement and scale-out. In multi-LoRA serving, the least-loaded replica may still be adapter-cold, while a moderately loaded replica can offer lower TTFT if the target adapter is already GPU-ready or close to execution. PrimeLoRA therefore uses adapter readiness as the dominant routing signal and applies lightweight runtime-load hints only as tie-breakers. Scale-out preparation complements routing by warming likely-needed adapters for newly activated replicas.

For each replica $i$ and adapter $a$, PrimeLoRA maintains an observable tiered preparation state:
\begin{equation}
\tau_i(a,t) \in \{\mathrm{GPU}, \mathrm{HOST}, \mathrm{NVMe}, \mathrm{REMOTE}\}.
\label{eq:adapter_tier}
\end{equation}

GPU, HOST, and NVMe denote local preparation tiers on the inference node. HOST is a host-memory-backed file tier, so adapters remain addressable as local files while being backed by DRAM. REMOTE denotes the backing artifact store and also covers a local miss, where the adapter is absent from the GPU, HOST, and NVMe hints of the selected replica. Thus, $\tau_i(a,t)$ captures the observable preparation distance from adapter $a$ to GPU execution on replica $i$, and serves as the shared state for routing and residency control.

Given a request $r$ with target adapter $a(r)$, PrimeLoRA does not route the request by queue length alone. It first derives a tier-induced readiness rank for each running replica:
\begin{equation}
\rho_i(a,t)=\kappa(\tau_i(a,t)),
\label{eq:readiness_rank}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa(\mathrm{GPU})>\kappa(\mathrm{HOST})>
\kappa(\mathrm{NVMe})>\kappa(\mathrm{REMOTE})$. For request $r$, PrimeLoRA then forms the highest-readiness candidate set:
\begin{equation}
I_r^+(t)=
\{i\in I_t \mid
\rho_i(a(r),t)=\rho_r^{\max}(t)\}
\label{eq:readiness_candidates}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\rho_r^{\max}(t)=
\max_{j\in I_t}\rho_j(a(r),t)
\label{eq:max_readiness}
\end{equation}

Among these candidates, PrimeLoRA selects the replica with the smallest runtime-load tuple:
\begin{equation}
i^*=
\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{i\in I_r^+(t)}
L_i(t),
\label{eq:placement_rule}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
L_i(t)=
(q_i(t),n_i(t),u_i(t),s_i(t),c_i).
\label{eq:load_tuple}
\end{equation}

Here, $q_i(t)$, $n_i(t)$, $u_i(t)$, $s_i(t)$, and $c_i$ denote the loading-queue depth, active request count, GPU-utilization hint, last-selection time, and replica creation time, respectively. PrimeLoRA compares $L_i(t)$ in tuple order: it first minimizes loading-queue depth and then uses the remaining fields as deterministic tie-breakers. Thus, runtime load is used only among replicas with the same adapter-readiness level.

This design differs from conventional adapter-affinity routing. A pure affinity policy may only record whether a replica has seen an adapter before, without distinguishing the adapter's current distance to GPU execution or the replica's load state. PrimeLoRA instead orders replicas by tier-induced readiness and uses load-aware tie-breaking only within the same readiness rank, avoiding idle but adapter-cold replicas and hot replicas whose hit benefit is offset by queueing or loading delay.

PrimeLoRA makes scaling decisions from three groups of runtime signals: arrival pressure, service saturation, and latency degradation. Arrival pressure is reflected by request rate and queue length, service saturation by resource utilization and busy-instance ratio, and latency degradation by response-time growth. Scale-out is triggered when aggregated scale-out evidence exceeds the configured threshold and the maximum replica count is not reached. Scale-in is triggered only when scale-in evidence remains high, no scale-out evidence is present, the minimum replica count is respected, and the cooldown window has elapsed. These conditions prevent transient bursts from causing oscillation while preserving responsiveness to sustained load changes.

Once scale-out is triggered, PrimeLoRA coordinates two readiness decisions: how much usable capacity the new replicas can provide under the GPU budget, and which adapters should be prepared for them. Rather than treating every launched runtime as immediately service-ready, PrimeLoRA estimates the demand likely to remain when candidate runtimes become ready, and assigns the handoff warmup budget to likely-needed adapters.

PrimeLoRA uses a readiness value-density term:
\begin{equation}
F_i(a,t)=
\frac{h_a(t)\Delta d_i(a,t)}{m_a}
\label{eq:adapter_value}
\end{equation}

Here, $h_a(t)$ denotes the online hotness of adapter $a$, $\Delta d_i(a,t)$ is the expected loading-delay reduction obtained by moving $a$ closer to GPU execution on replica $i$, and $m_a$ is the adapter size. Thus, $F_i(a,t)$ measures the expected readiness improvement per unit of residency budget.

PrimeLoRA implements this step as bounded online planning rather than closed-form optimization. The handoff budget $B_i^{\mathrm{ho}}$ limits warmup capacity, and candidates are ranked using recent access, online hotness, value per byte, local-tier availability, and adapter size. The selected adapters form the handoff set $P_i^{\mathrm{ho}}$.

The handoff set guides how a newly activated replica is prepared during its early service window. Adapters in $P_i^{\mathrm{ho}}$ are warmed first, so requests targeting these adapters are more likely to find the new replica service-ready. This biases early scale-out capacity toward adapters that can immediately contribute to serving residual demand.

\subsection{Hierarchical LoRA Adapter Residency and Dynamic Migration}

PrimeLoRA next controls adapter retention in a local three-tier residency hierarchy spanning GPU memory, the HOST tier, and local NVMe storage. The remote artifact storage node keeps the complete adapter pool and serves as the backing store for adapters not available locally, while NVMe, HOST, and GPU provide progressively faster local access paths on the inference node. This hierarchy exposes the preparation state $\tau_i(a,t)$ to the router and allows the residency manager to decide where each adapter should be retained or moved next.

For each adapter $a$, PrimeLoRA maintains metadata including its size $m_a$, current tier $\tau_i(a,t)$, recent access hotness, estimated loading time, and value per byte. The readiness value of moving $a$ closer to execution is captured by $F_i(a,t)$ in Eq.~\ref{eq:adapter_value}. The residency manager reuses this value for preloading, promotion, and replacement while enforcing tier capacity, safety margins, and eviction constraints, keeping routing, scale-out preparation, and residency control aligned around the same readiness objective.

For a target tier $\ell \in \{\mathrm{GPU}, \mathrm{HOST}, \mathrm{NVMe}\}$, PrimeLoRA constructs a candidate pool $U_i^{\ell}(t)$ from adapters that currently reside in slower tiers and satisfy minimum hotness and value thresholds. At the design level, migration can be abstracted as selecting a high-value set under the available tier budget:
\begin{equation}
M_i^{\ell}(t)=
\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{M\subseteq U_i^{\ell}(t)}
\sum_{a\in M} F_i(a,t)
\label{eq:residency_selection}
\end{equation}
subject to
\begin{equation}
\sum_{a\in M} m_a \le B_i^{\ell}(t)
\label{eq:residency_budget}
\end{equation}

Here, $M_i^{\ell}(t)$ is the adapter set selected for tier $\ell$, and $B_i^{\ell}(t)$ is the available tier budget after reserved capacity and safety margins are applied. PrimeLoRA realizes this objective with a bounded online planner: it filters lower-tier candidates by hotness, value per byte, and size constraints, additionally requiring recent access during scale-out; ranks the remaining candidates by a configured priority over value per byte, online hotness, predicted loading time, and tier-upgrade benefit; and then uses MiB-granularity 0--1 knapsack when the plan fits the dynamic-programming budget, otherwise scanning candidates by priority until the tier budget is exhausted.

PrimeLoRA applies this selection principle in two paths. During scale-out and background preparation, the planner moves selected lower-tier adapters toward the target tier under the tier budget. During online migration, repeated NVMe hits can trigger HOST promotion, and hot HOST- or NVMe-tier adapters can become GPU-admission candidates. If the target tier lacks space, replacement is admitted only when the incoming adapter has higher value than the resident candidate, avoiding stale retention and indiscriminate promotion.

The resulting hierarchy reduces repeated cross-tier fetching and exposes adapter locality to hit-aware routing, while leaving momentary GPU admission to the resource coordinator when KV-cache or batching pressure is high. This separation allows residency optimization to improve service readiness without assuming that every hot adapter should become GPU-resident immediately.

\subsection{Coordinated Resource Control for LoRA Loading and Inference}

The residency mechanism identifies which adapters are valuable to keep close to execution, but it does not by itself decide whether a GPU promotion is safe at a specific moment. During online serving, LoRA adapters, the KV cache, and batched execution share the same GPU memory and bandwidth budget. Promoting an adapter to GPU memory may reduce future misses, but the loading operation can also consume memory headroom and introduce transient I/O pressure. PrimeLoRA therefore coordinates adapter loading with the current inference state instead of treating it as an independent cache operation.

For each replica $i$, PrimeLoRA summarizes the current contention pressure as
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
p_i(t)=
\max\{&
p_i^{\mathrm{mem}}(t),
p_i^{\mathrm{KV}}(t), 
p_i^{\mathrm{predKV}}(t),
p_i^{\mathrm{load}}(t)
\}
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:resource_pressure}
\end{equation}

The four terms capture available-memory pressure, current KV-cache pressure, predicted near-term KV growth, and concurrent adapter-loading pressure. This conservative aggregation blocks proactive loading when any shared resource becomes a bottleneck. The recent adapter working-set gap is handled separately as a demand signal for forward migration, rather than as additional memory pressure.

PrimeLoRA then computes the effective GPU capacity available for proactive adapter admission:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
E_i(t)=
\max\{0,\,
&[M_i^{\mathrm{avail}}(t)
-\widehat{M}_i^{\mathrm{KV}}(t)] \cdot [1-p_i(t)]\}
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:effective_capacity}
\end{equation}

Here, $M_i^{\mathrm{avail}}(t)$ is the adapter-admission memory remaining after model weights, current KV cache, resident LoRA adapters, and the loading reserve are excluded. $\widehat{M}_i^{\mathrm{KV}}(t)$ is the predicted near-term KV-cache growth, and $p_i(t)$ is the contention pressure in Eq.~\ref{eq:resource_pressure}. Thus, $E_i(t)$ denotes the pressure-adjusted capacity for proactive GPU promotion without crowding out KV-cache growth or batched inference.
For a candidate adapter $a$, PrimeLoRA computes an admission value:
\begin{equation}
v_i(a,t)=h_a(t)\phi(\tau_i(a,t)),
\label{eq:admission_value}
\end{equation}
where $h_a(t)$ is the recent hotness of adapter $a$, and $\phi(\tau_i(a,t))$ is a locality factor determined by the source tier. PrimeLoRA also measures the normalized working-set gap $w_i(t)$, which indicates how much recent adapter demand is not yet covered by GPU residency. The admission score is
\begin{equation}
\sigma_i(a,t)=v_i(a,t)+w_i(t)-p_i(t).
\label{eq:admission_score}
\end{equation}

PrimeLoRA admits proactive GPU promotion only when
$\sigma_i(a,t)>0
\quad \mathrm{and} \quad
m_a \le E_i(t).$
If either condition fails, the adapter remains serviceable from the nearest available local tier, and explicit GPU residency is deferred until the pressure state improves. This controlled deferral prevents locality improvement from shifting the bottleneck to in-flight inference under bursty load.

This admission rule separates readiness value from safe execution: the residency manager identifies adapters worth moving closer to execution, while the resource coordinator decides whether the current runtime can absorb the movement. During scale-in, PrimeLoRA does not discard all adapter state with the removed capacity. It retains a bounded warm pool of adapters with high expected reuse and reload cost when doing so does not block the inference working set. These controls reduce readiness delay across scale-out and scale-in without shifting the bottleneck to foreground inference.

\section{Implementation}

We implement PrimeLoRA as a Python-based elastic serving control layer on top of vLLM 0.10.2~\cite{vLLM}, using PEFT-format LoRA adapters with PyTorch/Transformers support. PrimeLoRA leaves vLLM's attention kernels, continuous batching, and tensor-parallel execution unchanged, and adds a control plane for adapter-aware placement, preparation, residency tracking, and GPU admission around vLLM replicas. The prototype contains 16.9K non-comment, non-blank SLOC for the runtime and control plane, plus 18.3K SLOC for experiment and reproducibility scripts.

\textbf{Replica runtime and control plane.}
Each PrimeLoRA replica is a Python worker process that hosts one vLLM \texttt{AsyncLLMEngine} with LoRA support enabled. For each request, the controller selects a target replica, resolves the requested adapter to a backend-addressable local path, and invokes vLLM through its \texttt{LoRARequest} interface. Token generation, KV-cache management, batching, CUDA kernels, and tensor parallelism are handled by vLLM. The PrimeLoRA control plane manages autoscaling, replica lifecycle, adapter registry, preloading, residency state, scheduling, and GPU admission. In the prototype, Eq.~\ref{eq:readiness_rank} is implemented with per-replica adapter-affinity hints. Eq.~\ref{eq:load_tuple} uses loading-queue depth, active requests, GPU-utilization hints, and selection timestamps. Eq.~\ref{eq:admission_score} is evaluated before proactive GPU promotion using adapter hotness, source-tier locality, KV footprint, predicted KV growth, and loading pressure.

\textbf{Adapter residency and preparation.}
PrimeLoRA realizes the four preparation states in Eq.~\ref{eq:adapter_tier} through the local residency hierarchy and the remote artifact store. In our reported configuration, HOST is implemented as a DRAM-backed tmpfs layer, allowing adapters to remain addressable as local files while being physically backed by host memory. Adapter preparation runs asynchronously during scale-out and background residency updates. The preloading component materializes selected adapters into the nearest feasible local tier and records successful movements in the registry. For GPU admission, PrimeLoRA prepares a local adapter path and uses vLLM's LoRA loading interface rather than maintaining a separate GPU-resident adapter format.

\textbf{Resource coordination and instrumentation.}
Before admitting a proactive GPU promotion, PrimeLoRA checks whether the adapter fits within the current GPU-memory budget after accounting for model weights, expected KV-cache growth, resident adapters, and concurrent loading pressure. Unsafe promotions are deferred rather than allowed to interfere with active inference. PrimeLoRA instruments request arrival, dispatch/admission, adapter resolution, first-token generation, and request completion. These hooks support runtime control and mechanism-level analysis without changing vLLM's core execution path.

\section{Evaluation and Analysis}
\label{sec:evaluation}

\subsection{Evaluation Setup}

\textbf{Experimental Equipment.}
We evaluate PrimeLoRA on a two-node testbed. The GPU inference node runs Ubuntu 24.04.3 LTS with two Intel Xeon Silver CPUs, 125\,GB memory, four NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPUs (24\,GB each), and a 3.3\,TB RAID-backed local disk. The remote artifact storage node runs Ubuntu 24.04.3 LTS with two Intel Xeon Silver 4210R CPUs (40 logical cores), 125\,GB memory, 8\,GB swap, multiple RAID-backed disks, and four 1\,Gbps Ethernet interfaces.

\textbf{Models and LoRA Adapters.}
Unless otherwise stated, the reported main results use Llama-2-7B as the representative backbone. This setting is the currently closed five-system workload and uses tensor parallelism degree 1. Larger-backbone robustness uses the same adapter-pool construction for Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct, and Llama-2-13B when those rounds are reported, with tensor parallelism degree 2 for the 13B/14B models. For each backbone, we use a frozen PEFT-format pool of 500 LoRA adapters, sanitized before evaluation and heterogeneous in rank, size, task/domain label, and metadata. All systems in the same round use the same sampled adapter subset, so performance differences do not come from different adapter universes. All adapters and metadata are stored on the remote artifact node and are fetched on demand into local NVMe storage, the HOST tier, and GPU memory. The inference stack uses Python 3.12, PyTorch 2.8.0+cu128, vLLM 0.10.2, Transformers 4.57.6, and PEFT.

\textbf{Datasets and Workloads.}
We construct workloads from Azure LLM Inference Trace and ShareGPT. Azure LLM Inference Trace provides request timestamps and input/output token counts for conversation and code services; the released slice used in our implementation contains about 28K requests~\cite{DynamoLLM}. ShareGPT provides realistic prompt text, from which we extract the first human turn to build a prompt pool of up to 5,000 records~\cite{EfficientLLMScheduling}. We replay Azure arrivals and token statistics, and assign prompts sampled from ShareGPT. This preserves realistic inter-arrival patterns, token-length distributions, and burstiness while avoiding template-driven prompts~\cite{BurstGPT}.

Each formal run contains 4,000 requests. We uniformly rescale the Azure inter-arrival times and use the same replay timeline for all systems, preserving the relative arrival order and burst structure while keeping the workload within the operating range of the four-GPU testbed. We use the mixed Azure trace of conversation and code requests, and every evaluated request is associated with a target LoRA adapter. Target adapters are sampled from a Zipf distribution with exponent 1.0 over a pool of 500 adapters~\cite{Punica}. To model non-stationary adapter popularity, we periodically rotate a bounded active adapter hot set every 500 requests.

\textbf{Compared Systems.}
We compare PrimeLoRA with four representative serving systems under the same request trace, prompt pool, adapter pool, token budget, and hardware configuration.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{vLLM}~\cite{vLLM} is a high-throughput LLM serving runtime built around PagedAttention and continuous batching. It serves as the standalone execution-runtime baseline.
    \item \textbf{SGLang}~\cite{SGLang} is an efficient serving runtime for structured language model programs, with runtime optimizations such as KV-cache reuse and efficient decoding support. We use its serving runtime with LoRA support as a strong serverful baseline under the same shared trace, adapter subset, and GPU budget.
    \item \textbf{SLoRA}~\cite{SLoRA} is a multi-LoRA serving system that manages many adapters through unified paging and loads adapters from host memory to GPU memory on demand. It represents a host-memory-centric adapter management baseline.
    \item \textbf{ServerlessLLM}~\cite{ServerlessLLM} is a serverless LLM serving system that reduces startup overhead through multi-tier checkpoint loading, live migration, and startup-aware scheduling. It represents a startup-aware serverless serving baseline.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Ablations.}
We use cumulative ablations to isolate the marginal contribution of each implemented PrimeLoRA mechanism and map the results to the three motivation problems. PrimeLoRA-NVMe enables hit-aware local preparation, PrimeLoRA-NoCoord adds residency and migration without coordinated GPU admission, and full PrimeLoRA adds resource coordination to address contention between adapter loading and online inference.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{PrimeLoRA-NVMe} enables hit-aware adapter preparation into local NVMe storage. It evaluates whether local preparation reduces remote-readiness delay under the shared replay.
    \item \textbf{PrimeLoRA-NoCoord} further enables adapter residency and migration without coordinated GPU admission. It evaluates whether residency alone improves readiness and where it can still create admission or interference pressure.
    \item \textbf{PrimeLoRA} enables all three mechanisms by adding Coordinated Resource Control for LoRA Loading and Inference. The comparison between \textbf{PrimeLoRA-NoCoord} and full \textbf{PrimeLoRA} isolates the marginal effect of coordinating adapter movement with KV-cache usage and batched inference.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Evaluation Metrics.}
We report the following primary metrics in the main evaluation table.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{TTFT.} We report average and p95 time-to-first-token, measured from scheduled replay arrival to the first output token or first-token event observed by the replay harness. When reliable server-side first-token timestamps are available, we use them; otherwise, we use the first streamed token observed by the client-side replay. This metric captures first-token responsiveness and is widely used to characterize prefill-side latency in interactive LLM serving~\cite{DistServe}.
    \item \textbf{End-to-end latency.} We report average and p95 end-to-end latency. End-to-end latency is measured from request arrival to request completion, and reflects the full user-perceived request latency.
    \item \textbf{TPOT.} We report both average and p95 time-per-output-token over generated tokens after the first token, using only requests with observed decode timing. TPOT reflects decode-stage efficiency after the first-token path has completed, and is commonly used together with TTFT to characterize serving latency across prefill and decode phases~\cite{DistServe}.
    \item \textbf{Token throughput.} We report aggregate token throughput in tokens per second. This metric measures the serving capacity achieved under the same workload and runtime configuration, following common LLM serving evaluations~\cite{vLLM}.
    \item \textbf{Cost per request.} We report average per-request monetary cost using a lifecycle-based cost model shared by all systems. The model follows public cloud active/idle GPU billing semantics and uses an idle-ready GPU factor of \(0.5/2.1=0.238\), derived from Alibaba Cloud Function Compute's public Tesla GPU CU conversion factors~\cite{AlibabaFCBilling,AlibabaFCIdleGPU}.
    \item \textbf{Cost efficiency.} We report CE = \(1/(\overline{L}_{\mathrm{E2E}}\cdot \overline{C})\), where \(\overline{L}_{\mathrm{E2E}}\) is the average end-to-end latency in seconds and \(\overline{C}\) is the lifecycle-based cost per completed request defined above. A higher CE indicates lower latency and lower serving cost under the same workload~\cite{ServerlessLoRA}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

Recent LLM serving systems improve runtime execution, multi-LoRA adapter serving, and serverless startup or scaling. PrimeLoRA builds on these advances but focuses on the gap between replica availability and adapter executability.

\subsection{Online LLM Serving Systems}

Online LLM serving systems improve serving efficiency through batching, KV-cache management, phase disaggregation, request scheduling, and runtime-level optimization. vLLM~\cite{vLLM} introduces PagedAttention to manage KV cache at page granularity and improve memory utilization under continuous batching. SGLang~\cite{SGLang} provides an efficient serving runtime for structured language model programs and optimizes execution through runtime scheduling and reuse-oriented serving primitives. Orca~\cite{Orca} uses iteration-level scheduling and selective batching to reduce queueing overhead. DistServe~\cite{DistServe} disaggregates prefill and decode across GPUs and jointly provisions resources under TTFT and TPOT constraints to reduce cross-phase interference. Splitwise~\cite{Splitwise} also separates prefill and decode across machines to enable phase-specific resource provisioning. Llumnix~\cite{Llumnix} improves load balance and tail latency through runtime rescheduling and state migration. Mooncake~\cite{MooncakeFAST25} builds a KV-cache-centric disaggregated serving architecture that incorporates host memory and local storage into a unified cache path. SCORPIO~\cite{SCORPIO} further targets heterogeneous service objectives by jointly considering TTFT and TPOT in request admission, queue management, and batch formation.

\subsection{Multi-LoRA LLM Serving Systems}

In the shared-backbone setting, prior work on multi-LoRA inference treats adapters as first-class serving objects and optimizes their loading, caching, batching, and switching overheads. Punica~\cite{Punica} introduces specialized kernels to batch requests across different LoRA adapters while sharing a single backbone model, improving both GPU compute efficiency and memory utilization. SLoRA~\cite{SLoRA} stores adapters in host memory and transfers them to GPU memory on demand, while jointly managing adapter weights and the KV cache through unified paging to reduce fragmentation and support many concurrent adapters. dLoRA~\cite{dLoRA} further couples request orchestration with adapter orchestration, using dynamic merge/unmerge and cross-replica migration of both requests and adapters to improve serving efficiency. Chameleon~\cite{Chameleon} introduces adapter caching and adapter-aware scheduling, retaining hot adapters in idle GPU memory and using multi-queue scheduling to mitigate head-of-line blocking under heterogeneous requests. ELORA~\cite{FASTLIBRA} places LoRA adapters and the KV cache into a shared cache pool and reduces TTFT through dependency-aware management and performance-driven swapping.

\subsection{Serverless LLM Inference Systems}

Serverless LLM inference systems focus on startup latency, model loading, and elastic scaling. ServerlessLLM~\cite{ServerlessLLM} addresses large checkpoint size, slow remote fetching, and underutilized local hierarchical storage by introducing a load-optimized checkpoint format, multi-tier local checkpoint loading, live migration, and startup-latency-aware scheduling. Medusa~\cite{MedusaServerless} uses state materialization to move pre-execution steps such as model initialization, tokenizer preparation, and computation graph construction to an offline stage, and restores them at runtime to shorten the cold-start critical path. HydraServe~\cite{HydraServeNSDI26} leverages pipeline parallelism to distribute the loading process of a single replica across multiple GPU servers, and combines parallelism selection, placement optimization, and pipeline reclamation to reduce cold-start latency and SLO violations. $\lambda$Scale~\cite{LambdaScale} targets rapid scale-out under bursty workloads using high-speed multicast and execute-while-load, reducing the time required for new instances to become service-ready. ServerlessLoRA~\cite{ServerlessLoRA} further shows that, in LoRA-based serving, auxiliary artifacts such as adapters, libraries, and kernels can also lie on the TTFT-critical path, and reduces latency and cost through backbone sharing, artifact preloading, contention-aware batching, and dynamic offloading. PrimeLoRA addresses a complementary runtime-control problem: it coordinates adapter readiness across elastic replicas, request placement, tiered residency, and GPU admission under shifting adapter hotness.

\subsection{Summary of Related Work}

Prior work has improved runtime execution, multi-LoRA adapter serving, and serverless startup or artifact loading. These mechanisms are essential, but they do not fully couple replica activation with adapter executability under bursty multi-LoRA demand. As a result, adapter lookup, cross-tier movement, and GPU admission may still appear on the TTFT-critical path after scale-out. PrimeLoRA targets this remaining gap by treating adapter readiness as a service-path property and coordinating routing, scale-out preparation, hierarchical residency, and GPU admission under dynamic adapter hotness.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper presented PrimeLoRA, a scaling-aware serverless system for multi-LoRA LLM inference. PrimeLoRA reduces the service-readiness gap between elastic replica activation and adapter execution by aligning request placement, scale-out preparation, hierarchical adapter residency, and GPU-admission control around adapter readiness. We implement PrimeLoRA on a multi-GPU prototype and evaluate it with trace-driven multi-LoRA workloads. The results show that PrimeLoRA improves lifecycle cost efficiency and reduces readiness-related delay, while exposing the expected latency tradeoff against always-on serverful runtimes. As future work, we plan to extend PrimeLoRA to deployments with multiple inference nodes and broader multimodal serving scenarios.
